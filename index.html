The big idea

Technology changes faster than our intuitions do.

As technology and ideas progress, society evolves to take advantage of new media, economic, and technological landscapes. In a single human lifetime we went from a world where creating and distributing media was an expensive, tightly controlled endeavor to one where anyone can publish a blog, video, or podcast for almost nothing.

The central pursuit of this course is an investigation of the economies of scale and advances in technology that now make it good business to give away services for free — and an attempt, by students, to envision and create the direction (or possible directions) society will evolve next.

What students actually do

Read the landscape. Then predict it.

🌌

Trace complexity

Using the lens of Big History, students chart how the universe — and then human society — crossed thresholds of rising complexity, from the Big Bang to collective learning to the internet.

💸

Decode the economy of free

Students learn core microeconomics — markets, supply and demand, marginal cost — then study how "free" was transformed inside a digital "bits" economy and how the big tech ecosystems exploit it.

🔮

Make a disciplined prediction

Each student picks a domain they care about, builds a punctuated-equilibrium model of its past and present, and writes an evidence-based argument for its future.

The signature tool

The punctuated-equilibrium diagram.

The course's recurring analytical move is a diagram with time on the x-axis and complexity on the y-axis. Complexity doesn't rise smoothly — it sits on long plateaus broken by sudden jumps, each triggered by a new technology, a falling cost, or a new exchange network. Students use the curve to analyze the past and to structure their predictions of the future.

Whose idea this is. The punctuated-equilibrium model of technological change — and this diagram — is the course's own framework, developed by Jonathan Briggs and Matt Delaney. It shares some underlying vocabulary with the Big History Project (Goldilocks conditions, thresholds of complexity, collective learning), but Big History stops short of this synthesis. Turning those ideas into a single predictive tool for reading and forecasting technology is original to this course.

Philosophy & goals

Predicting the future is a skill you can practice.

Students grow up inside technological change, but rarely get tools to think about it structurally. This course treats that as a teachable skill. It is deliberately interdisciplinary — part history, part economics, part technology, part media studies — because the forces it studies don't respect subject boundaries.

The method is consistent across every unit: study how change has happened, name the mechanisms that drove it, then use those mechanisms to reason about what happens next. Problem-solving and flexible thinking are valued over memorization. The goal is not for students to be right about the future — it's for them to build arguments about the future that are honest, evidence-based, and falsifiable.

The recurring question: If history helps us avoid repeating the mistakes of the past, what happens when the landscape of the future stops resembling the past at all?
Essential questions

The questions students wrestle with all term.

?

How does the existence of almost-free digital memory in a "bits" economy change economic interaction and the spread of technological tools?

?

If history helps us avoid past mistakes, what happens when the landscape of the future ceases to match that of the past?

?

When all facts and figures are instantly retrievable, is there still value in knowing anything — and what will define a "knowledgeable" person in the future?

?

Would a massive increase in human longevity be sustainable under our current economic system? If not, what would have to change?

Learning goals

What students leave with.

  • A model of change. They can use punctuated equilibrium — thresholds, plateaus, and jumps in complexity — to describe how systems evolve over time.
  • Economic literacy for a digital world. They understand markets, supply and demand, and marginal cost, and can explain why so much of the digital economy is given away for free.
  • A critical read on big tech. They can analyze the business models and ecosystems of the dominant technology companies rather than just using their products.
  • Evidence-based argument. They can synthesize multiple sources, quote and cite them, and build a clear thesis about a contested question.
  • Disciplined speculation. They can make a prediction about the future that is specific, grounded in trends, and open to being wrong.
  • Collaborative knowledge-building. They can research in teams, aggregate findings, and teach what they've learned to peers.
Curriculum · the three modules

Then to now to next.

Three modules move students from the deep history of complexity, through the economics of the digital present, to a structured forecast of the future. Each carries equal weight in the gradebook.

Module1

Then to Now: the Big Bang to the Internet

An overview of history from the start of the universe to our modern, technological society, and an exploration of what the internet actually is — its architecture and how it works.

  • Big History framing: thresholds of increasing complexity, from cosmology to life to humans.
  • Collective learning and exchange networks as the engine of human acceleration.
  • The "mini-thresholds of life" and the rapid acceleration of recent history.
  • How the internet works, and the open-source community as a case study in non-market exchange.
Collective learningThresholds of complexityExchange networksOpen source
Module2

The Economy of Free & Other Disruptive Innovations

An introduction to core microeconomics — perfect markets, supply, demand, marginal cost — and how those concepts behave in technological development, followed by an analysis of how "free" has been transformed inside the digital "bits" economy.

  • Microeconomic foundations applied to technology and information goods.
  • The four models of "free" (drawn from Chris Anderson's Free): direct cross-subsidies, the three-party market, freemium, and non-monetary economies.
  • Why digital goods trend toward "too cheap to matter," and what demonetization does to industries.
  • Culminating study: the ecosystems of the big five tech companies — Amazon, Apple, Facebook (Meta), Google/Alphabet, and Microsoft.
Marginal cost4 models of freeFreemiumBig 5 ecosystems
Module3

Emerging Trends: the Tech Landscape

A discussion of how technological innovation and the societal trends it produces are reshaping daily life — and where their long-term path leads. This module turns the analytical tools loose on the future.

  • Surveying the current landscape: AI and machine learning, AR/VR, automation and the future of work, longevity, and the manipulation of reality.
  • The accelerating power of technology (Kurzweil) and what exponential change implies.
  • Students select a domain and build a punctuated-equilibrium argument for its future.
  • A final "exchange," where the class shares and pressure-tests one another's predictions.

A sample flow

One trimester, week by week.

A representative pacing for a ~10-week trimester. Treat it as a skeleton — the modules are modular by design, and the adaptation guide shows how to stretch or compress this.

Rhythm: most weeks pair a reading-and-post for homework with a collaborative in-class activity (case studies, group research, diagramming), building toward the module's major assessment.
  • WEEKS 1–2 · MODULE 1

    Complexity & thresholds

    Big History readings and the "mini-thresholds of life." Students post reflections and build their first punctuated-equilibrium diagrams.

  • WEEK 3 · MODULE 1

    Collective learning & the internet

    Exchange networks, rapid acceleration, and how the internet works. Open source as a case study.

  • WEEK 4 · MODULE 1 → 2

    Synthesis diagram

    Groups produce the punctuated-equilibrium synthesis presentation — the Module 1 capstone.

  • WEEKS 5–6 · MODULE 2

    Microeconomics & the four models of free

    Markets, marginal cost, and Anderson's Free. Students post on demonetization and "too cheap to matter."

  • WEEK 7 · MODULE 2

    The Big 5 deep dive

    Groups each take one tech giant, research its ecosystem, and present. Paired with the "I cut X out of my life" readings.

  • WEEK 8 · MODULE 3

    Scanning the landscape

    Articles and videos on AI, AR/VR, automation, and longevity. Students choose their prediction domain.

  • WEEK 9 · MODULE 3

    Drafting "The Future of…"

    Domain & impact statements, outlines, peer editing, and production of the research paper and diagram.

  • WEEK 10 · FINAL

    The Final Exchange

    Students present predictions, record peers' forecasts in the prediction table, and complete the final synthesis.

Concept toolkit

The vocabulary students build.

Punctuated equilibrium

Long stable plateaus interrupted by rapid jumps. The course's master diagram: complexity (y) over time (x).

Collective learning

Humanity's unique ability to share and accumulate knowledge across generations — the driver of accelerating complexity.

Exchange networks

The trade, communication, and information links that connect groups and speed up innovation.

Marginal cost

The cost of producing one more unit. Near zero for digital goods — the economic root of "free."

The four models of free

Cross-subsidy, third-party (advertising), freemium, and non-monetary — how giving things away can be good business.

Disruptive innovation

A new entrant that reshapes a market by changing its economics, not just improving the product.

Short on time?

The one-class-period version.

You don't need a whole trimester to use the central idea. The punctuated-equilibrium concept works as a single, self-contained lesson — a way to give students a durable model of how complex systems change in one sitting. Below is the exact shape of a mock class period built around it.

With gratitude to the Big History Project. Some of the vocabulary in this lesson — Goldilocks conditions, thresholds of increasing complexity, collective learning, and the framing of change across the universe and human culture — is adapted from the Big History Project, whose free curriculum we gratefully credit. The punctuated-equilibrium model itself — applying that cycle to technology to actually predict what comes next — is this course's own contribution, and any derivative should carry both acknowledgments.

The session, start to finish

  • OPEN

    Warm-up prompt

    With a partner, identify a time when something — in your life or that you observed — evolved or changed rapidly, becoming more complex. Students share their "paradigm shift" as they introduce themselves.

  • FRAME

    Context setting

    The teacher frames the day: we're going to build one model — punctuated equilibrium — for how complex systems evolve, and then test it on a real story.

  • READ

    Shared text

    The class reads a short common text (here, the rapid shift to remote learning during the 2020–21 pandemic) that everyone will analyze through the model.

  • DEFINE

    Definitions

    Students receive definition cards for the core terms — Goldilocks conditions, threshold moments, acceleration, old & new paradigms / plateaus, feedback loops.

  • GROUP

    Group processing

    In groups of ~4, each group makes sure everyone understands their assigned definition, then hunts for places in the shared text where that concept appears.

  • SHARE

    Groups share out

    Each group teaches the rest of the class where they found their concept at work — building the full diagram collectively.

  • DISCUSS

    Discussion

    The class connects the pieces into one punctuated-equilibrium curve and debates where the next threshold might be.

  • CLOSE

    Conclusion

    Students make a prediction about what comes next — and note what evidence would tell them whether they were right.

The worked example: how "remote school" evolved

The lesson uses one accessible story to make the abstract model concrete. Remote learning didn't appear overnight in 2020 — it crossed a series of thresholds, each enabled by a new technology, with long plateaus in between.

  • 1800s · THRESHOLD

    Correspondence courses

    The expanding postal service made "learning at a distance" possible — assignments mailed to professors, feedback mailed back. By the late 1800s many universities had correspondence departments.

  • 20TH CENTURY · THRESHOLD

    Radio & television

    Broadcast brought lectures to scale; coursework was still mailed in. (In many ways, the TED talk is a short-form descendant of this format.)

  • 1989–2008 · ACCELERATION

    The internet, LMSs & MOOCs

    Virtual colleges, then learning-management systems, then online video converge: massively open online courses can enroll thousands at once.

  • MARCH 2020 · RAPID JUMP

    The pandemic forcing function

    Video-conferencing adoption skyrockets in weeks. The share of people comfortable in an online meeting jumps from very few to most of the connected world — a textbook punctuated jump after a century of plateaus.

The closing challenge, in the course's own words: “What will be next? Who knows. I recommend you guess, write it down, and figure out what pieces of information you would have needed to get it right. Almost all predictions of the future are wrong — but some are less wrong than others.”
Assessments & grading

Equal weight across the arc.

Each module carries the same weight, with a small final synthesis at the end. This keeps students engaged across the whole term rather than betting everything on a final.

Module 2 — The Economy of Free30%Economics work, the Big 5 research & presentation

Final Synthesis10%Final exchange and reflection across the term

Signature projects

The four assessments that define the course.

Synthesis Diagram (presentation)

Module 1 capstone. In groups, students build a punctuated-equilibrium diagram with at least three steps — time on the x-axis, complexity on the y — that models either an analogy or a real progression toward complexity. Each member contributes one step, all key terms are labeled and connected to the scenario, and a final slide combines the three steps into one global diagram.

"The Future of…" Research Paper

The intellectual heart of Module 3. A short single-spaced paper in which students select 3–4 articles as foundation, analyze each with quotations, in-text citations, and commentary, and make a clear argument for a specific future trend in a domain of their choosing — accompanied by a labeled punctuated-equilibrium diagram capturing the past, present, and predicted future.

Big 5 Tech Companies (presentation)

Module 2's culminating group work. Each group takes one of Amazon, Apple, Facebook/Meta, Google/Alphabet, or Microsoft, researches its ecosystem and business model, and presents how it captures value — often in the digital economy through "free." Paired with the firsthand "I cut X out of my life" readings.

The Final Exchange (prediction table)

The course's closing ritual. Students present their forecasts and record every classmate's topic and prediction in a shared prediction table — turning individual research into a collective map of where the class thinks the world is headed.

Build your own version

Six planning moves.

  1. Keep the spine: then → now → next

    Whatever you change, preserve the three-part arc — deep history of complexity, the economics of the present, a forecast of the future — and the punctuated-equilibrium diagram as the connective tool. That structure is what makes the course coherent.

  2. Anchor each module to one idea and one artifact

    Module 1 → complexity, ending in the synthesis diagram. Module 2 → the economics of free, ending in the Big 5 study. Module 3 → the landscape, ending in the "Future of…" paper. Pick the single deliverable that proves the module, then build backward to it.

  3. Refresh the examples for this year

    Swap dated readings and companies for current ones. The framework is durable; the examples are disposable. Updating them is a yearly ritual, not a one-time fix.

  4. Tune the reading load to your students

    The original pairs a short read-and-post for homework with a hands-on activity in class. Scale the volume up or down, and lean on videos and annotated excerpts where a full text is too heavy.

  5. Set up a shared workspace and clear naming

    Create a class notebook or shared drive for readings, partnered notes, and final papers. Adopt the CW / HW / MA + bracketed-action naming so students always know the mode and the task at a glance.

  6. End with an exchange

    Close on the Final Exchange: students present forecasts and record each other's predictions in a shared table. It converts individual work into a collective artifact and gives the term a memorable ending.

Alternate formats

Fit it to your calendar & department.

Trimester (as-is)

~10 weeks, three modules of equal weight, one major deliverable each, closing in the Final Exchange.

Full semester

Add depth: a second economics unit, a primary-source history strand, or individual research checkpoints across Module 3. Add a midterm synthesis.

Single unit (4–5 weeks)

Run Module 2 alone as an "Economics of Free" unit, or Module 3 as a "Forecasting the Future" capstone in an existing course.

Humanities / social studies

Emphasize the Big History framing, media studies, and the argumentative paper. Lighten the microeconomics.

Economics elective

Deepen markets, marginal cost, and platform economics; use the tech landscape as applied case studies.

CS / interdisciplinary

Expand "how the internet works" and open source; pair the forecasting paper with a build or prototype.
